I recently watched _tick, tick…BOOM!_, a film about pursuing dreams while grappling with time, mortality, and the looming pressure of turning 30. It hit me on a personal level. I’m turning 30 this year, and I found myself relating to many of the struggles portrayed—concerns about whether I’m doing enough, whether I’ll ever create something meaningful, and whether I’ve made the right choices so far.

Turning 30 feels like a moment of self-evaluation. I’ve noticed that many people my age are already married or have kids. Some seem to have achieved what they set out to do, building businesses, making investments, or at least giving the impression that they have it all figured out. I know it’s not ideal to compare myself to others, but sometimes it feels like I’m behind. When I look back, I feel like I wasted a lot of time trying to prove something—trying to appear like I knew what I was doing.

I often buried myself in research, learning about topics so I could speak confidently about them when others brought them up. Deep down, I think I wanted to be seen, to be recognized—not just for doing what I love, but for being _someone_ who knows things. But now I wonder if that was driven by hubris or some inflated sense of self-worth.

Maybe it stems from childhood trauma. Maybe that's just an explanation I give myself—a way to justify my behavior or search for meaning in it. Whatever the reason, I’ve come to realize that there are always two forces at play:

- One is the awareness of our actions and motivations.
    
- The other is the acceptance that even if we understand why we are the way we are, it doesn’t change the past.
    

I’m now caught between seeking meaning in my past and learning to accept the decisions that brought me here.

One quote that resonates deeply with me is the Serenity Prayer:

> _“God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, the courage to change the things I can,  
> and the wisdom to know the difference.”_

There are things I regret, things I can’t undo. But I’m also learning to find the courage to change what I can right now. The hardest part, at least for me, is having the wisdom to know the difference—especially because I can be stubborn and prideful.

That brings me to humility.

### 🧭 What Is Humility, Really?

Humility, as I understand it, is the recognition and acceptance of reality, shaped by an open-minded pursuit of truth. It’s not something we automatically possess—it’s something we have to practice. And ironically, the moment we claim we are humble is often the moment we prove we’re not. Just like pride, humility is difficult to identify in ourselves—it often takes someone else to point it out.

For me, humility means having the courage to accept life’s constants, the strength to change what I _can_ influence, and the wisdom to admit that I don’t know everything. It’s planning for the future with the knowledge I currently have—while acknowledging the uncertainty that always surrounds us.

We don’t have to figure out the next year or the next decade.  
Sometimes, all we need to know is **the next step—and then the step after that**.
